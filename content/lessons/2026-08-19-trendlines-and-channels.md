## Trendlines and channels: drawing rules, breakouts

A trendline is a straight line connecting consecutive swing highs or lows to show the direction and slope of a trend. A channel is two parallel lines (a trendline plus its mirror) boxing price into a predictable band. Trendlines turn the noisy idea of "direction" into something you can draw, test, and trade mechanically. They work because they mark where the crowd has repeatedly placed orders and where expectations cluster, not because lines have any inherent magic.

## Key Concepts
- **Uptrend line**: connect at least two rising swing lows; price should keep finding support here. The more touches, the more valid (3+ is solid; a line with 2 touches is only a draft).
- **Downtrend line**: connect successive falling swing highs; price keeps getting rejected under it. Same validation rule applies.
- **Drawing rules (the gold standard)**: use candle bodies or wicks consistently (do not mix them), connect actual swing points, and let the line dictate touches - do not bend it to fit price. Touching wicks but not bodies is a common warning the line is wrong.
- **Channels**: the third touch on the opposite side confirms a valid channel. Ascending channel = higher lows + higher highs (uptrend), descending = lower highs + lower lows (downtrend), horizontal = range/consolidation.
- **A trendline is only propped by two points; the third touch confirms it.** Never trade a line on just two touches.
- **Angle matters**: overly steep lines (near vertical) break constantly and mean little; ~30-45 degree slopes are the most durable and tradeable.

## Reading it on a chart
- Draw the line before price arrives at it. If price approaches and bounces cleanly off it with good volume and a rejection wick, the trend is intact and the line is doing its job.
- In an uptrend, longs enter on the pullback to the ascending trendline; shorts enter on the bounce down into a descending trendline in a downtrend. This "buy the dip on the line" approach gives you better entries than chasing.
- Broaden a single line into a channel when you find the parallel boundary: price oscillating between the two lines gives you a range to trade (buy support of channel, sell resistance of channel in a range; or ride the outside in a strong trend).
- Spot potential set-ups by watching confluence: a trendline meeting support/resistance, a moving average, or a Fibonacci level at the same price is far more meaningful than any line alone.
- Confirmation of a break: a candle CLOSE beyond the line (not just a wick poke) plus rising volume, then a successful retest of the broken line in the new direction.

## Applying it to signals
- **Trade with the trendline, not against it.** In an uptrend with a rising line, leaning long on pullbacks to the line tilts your buy signals toward higher probability; trying to short the top of an intact uptrend fights the structure.
- **Downtrend line = filter for shorts.** Only take short signals when price is still respecting the descending line; a break above it is your first warning that bears are losing control and short entries should be skipped.
- **Use the line for stop placement.** A natural stop sits just beyond the trendline/channel boundary - a clean break of the line invalidates the thesis, so place stops there rather than an arbitrary distance.
- **Breakout = trend acceleration or reversal.** In an ascending channel, an upside breakout signals a faster leg up (ride it); a break DOWN through the lower channel line signals a reversal to the downside (flip to bearish). Use the parallel line depth to estimate the breakout target: many traders project the channel height from the break point.
- **Combine with the risk lesson**: channel trades give you a defined stop (behind the line) and a defined target (opposite boundary or breakout projection), so you can always compute R:R before you enter.

## Common Pitfalls
- Trading a trendline drawn through only two points - it has no confirmation yet.
- Drawing the line after the fact so it perfectly fits the chart; that is curve-fitting, not analysis.
- Following steep, near-vertical lines that break on routine pullbacks; the angle is telling you the move is exhausted.
- Calling a wick poke beyond the line a "breakout"; only a candle close with volume counts.
- Ignoring a retest: buying the breakout immediately at the line instead of waiting for a confirmed close and retest, which filters most false breakouts (fakeouts).
- Being too rigid - a line is a zone, so price can probe (wick) through it and still be a valid touch; expecting an exact tick is a recipe for being stopped out and watching price bounce.
